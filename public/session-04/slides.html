The Transportation Model

One variable per lane

x[p, w] = cases shipped from plant p to warehouse w

min  Σ c[p,w] · x[p,w]
Σw x[p,w] ≤ supply[p]   each plant — rows cap
Σp x[p,w] = demand[w]  each warehouse — columns fill

12 variables, 7 constraints. The same grammar as Session 2 — decide, aim, obey — but the decisions now live on a map.

Unbalanced Networks

Supply 800, demand 750: someone idles

  • Supply > demand: write supply as ≤ and the model chooses who idles (Albany, 50 cases)
  • Portland grows to 230 → total demand 800 = supply. Cost $2,530, every plant runs full
  • Portland at 240 → demand 810 > 800. The solver says INFEASIBLE — it will not lie to you
  • Fix: a dummy "shortage" source with a penalty cost. The model then tells you which city goes short
Transshipment · The One New Idea

At a hub, in equals out

Σ flow into hub  −  Σ flow out of hub  =  0

sources: net outflow = supply  ·  sinks: net inflow = demand  ·  hubs: zero

One constraint type, three node roles. Add cross-docks, distribution centers, ports — the grammar never changes. This is the constraint Anna Chakra writes at every state warehouse.

Integer Answers For Free

Ship 3.7 trucks? Never.

  • Every network constraint matrix is totally unimodular: each column has one +1 and one −1
  • So every corner of the feasible region is integer — whenever supplies and demands are integers
  • The LP is the integer program. No branch-and-bound. No extra compute. Even at 810 million people
  • Providence 150.5 → shipments of 150.5 and 9.5. The fraction came from the data, never from the model

Speed Round · Answers

Cost is a system property

Q1 · Infeasible. Supply 100 = demand 100 exactly. You can't sell what you can't make
Q2 · Store 3 at $6 / unit (S1 $4 · S2 $5 · S4 $5) — its cheapest lane is $4, but serving it cascades
Q3 · Albany $2 / unit · Hartford $1 · Boston $0 — Boston's lanes are already the last resort
Q4 · $450, +$40 a day. Boston → S4 goes 15 → 25, Hartford → S3 goes 5 → 25
Q5 · One dollar: Albany → S3 $4 → $5, or Hartford → S3 $5 → $4. Half the map re-routes

Bonus, the books: 25 × ($4 + $5 + $6 + $5) − (30 × $1 + 30 × $2) = $500 − $90 = $410. Demand values minus capacity values equals cost. Duality, on a map.

When An AI Writes Your Network Model

Two questions before you trust it

1 · Is it balanced?

Total supply vs total demand. An LLM will happily write supply as an equality on an unbalanced network — and hand you INFEASIBLE, or worse, a silently wrong dummy node.

2 · Do the prices make sense?

A city whose marginal cost is below its cheapest lane, or an idle plant with a positive shadow price, means a sign is flipped in the flow-balance constraint.

The model that feeds 810 million people is checked the same way. Structure first, prices second, code last.
