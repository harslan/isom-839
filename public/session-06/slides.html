Edelman Finalist · Procter & Gamble · North American Supply Chain

An LP can shrink a plant. It cannot close one.

What P&G asked

Dozens of plants, hundreds of products, thousands of customer zones. Which plants stay open, which close, and who makes what for whom?

Why an LP fails

Its answer is "run this plant at 42%." But a plant's fixed cost is paid in full whether it runs at 42% or 100%. The LP cannot see that cost.

What worked

A yes/no variable for each plant, and one constraint tying production to that yes/no. P&G closed about one plant in five and reported savings of about $200 million a year.

Tonight's five warehouses are the same model, small enough to solve in front of you.

The Model · Part 1 of the Notebook

Two kinds of decision, one model

open[w] ∈ {0, 1}
The yes/no decision. 1 if warehouse w opens, 0 if it does not.
x[w, c] ≥ 0
The how-much decision. Units per week sent from warehouse w to customer c.
min  Σ fixed[w] · open[w] + 52 · Σ ship[w,c] · x[w,c]
Total cost = leases for the warehouses we open + 52 weeks of shipping.
Σw x[w,c] = demand[c]
Every customer gets exactly what they ordered.

Something is missing. As written, the model can ship from a warehouse it never opened, and never pay the lease.

The Missing Constraint · The Fixed-Charge Link

If you ship from it, you pay for it

shipments out of w  ≤  capacity[w] · open[w]
If open = 0

The right side is zero. Nothing can leave the warehouse. As far as the model is concerned, it does not exist.

If open = 1

The right side is the capacity. Shipments can flow up to it, and the lease shows up in the total cost.

Why capacity, not a huge number

Textbooks call this multiplier "big M." The right M here is the capacity. A huge M makes the model much slower. More on that at the end.

This one line is the most useful pattern in the course. Every fixed-cost decision uses it.

How the Solver Searches · Schematic (top and bottom values are real, middle ones illustrative)

How the solver finds the best whole-number plan

Each box solves a relaxed problem. Its value is a floor for every box below it. Once a real plan at $295,800 exists, any box with a floor above that is thrown away without looking further. That is why 260 combinations can finish in a blink. Try it yourself in the Branch & Bound Explorer.

Notebook Part 4 · Why Yes/No Is Hard

Sixty yes/no decisions is more combinations than grains of sand on Earth

5 decisions → 32 combinations  ·  30 → a billion  ·  60 → 1.15 × 1018
The solver just proved the best of 1.15 × 1018 in a blink, gap 0.0%.
Why it is fast

The solver never checks every combination. The floors from the tree let it throw away most of the tree without looking.

When it is slow

A huge M makes the floors useless. Nothing gets thrown away. The same model can take hours.

What to report

The gap. A plan within 0.5% in ten seconds usually beats a perfect plan in ten hours. Say which one you delivered.

The AI Angle

Copilots write M = 1,000,000. You have to fix it.

  • An AI-drafted model with a huge M runs. But it solves slowly, or returns 0.9999 and calls it 1.
  • For every M, ask: what is the largest this sum could physically be? Usually a capacity, a demand, or a budget. Use that.
  • For every if-then and either/or rule, read it aloud as a sentence. If the business never said that sentence, delete the constraint.
  • Session 12 is a full lab on this. Start the habit tonight.
