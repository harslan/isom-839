Part 1 · Why We Needed It · Click To Reveal

The old way read one word at a time

Before 2017 · recurrent networks
📞 The telephone game

Read word 1, pass a summary to word 2, pass it on to word 3… By word 50, the start of the sentence is a faded memory.

Problem 1
🐌 Slow

Word 50 has to wait for words 1 to 49. You can't split the work across many chips.

Problem 2
🧠 Forgetful

Long-distance links get lost: "The keys that I left on the table next to the door are…"

2017: eight researchers at Google publish "Attention Is All You Need." Their idea: let every word look at every other word, all at once. They called it the transformer.

The telephone game is the key image: information passed along a chain degrades. The transformer removes the chain. Everything sees everything directly, and all at the same time, so it also runs fast on GPUs.
The Core Idea · Attention · Press The Buttons

What does "it" mean?

The percentages show how much the word "it" pays attention to each other word. (An illustration of the idea, not numbers from a real model.)

Press the two buttons. One changed word at the end ("tired" vs "wide") flips what "it" refers to: the animal or the street. You did that instantly by looking at the whole sentence. Attention is how a transformer does the same thing: every word decides which other words matter to it.
How Attention Works · Click To Reveal

A question, a name tag, and a message

1 · Query
❓ Every word asks a question

"it" asks: "Who am I referring to?"

2 · Key
🏷️ Every word wears a name tag

"animal" wears: "I'm a living thing that can get tired." The better a tag answers the question, the more attention it gets.

3 · Value
✉️ Every word carries a message

"it" collects the messages, weighted by attention (the weights add up to 100%), and updates what it means.

Like a meeting where everyone can hear everyone: you listen most to the people who answer your question.

Questions, tags and messages are all just lists of numbers, and the network learns how to write them during training. Several questions at once = "multi-head" attention.

Keep the real words (query, key, value) but teach the picture: question, name tag, message. Each word is a list of numbers; matching a question to a tag is a multiplication that gives a score; the scores become percentages. That is the whole mechanism.
The Whole Machine

Talk, think, repeat

Words become numbers + a position tag (1st, 2nd, 3rd…) so order isn't lost
Talk: attention. Every word gathers what it needs from the others.
Think: a small network (the Dense layers you built) updates each word on its own.
↻ repeat this pair many times (GPT-class models: dozens of layers)
Answer: a chance for every possible next word
What each round adds

Early layers: grammar. Which word goes with which.

Middle layers: meaning. "it" = the animal.

Late layers: the task. What should come next.

Each word starts as a dictionary entry and ends as that word in this sentence.

A transformer is a stack of two alternating steps: attention (words talk to each other) and a small feed-forward network (each word thinks alone). The early/middle/late description is a helpful simplification of what researchers observe, not a strict rule.

Why It's Useful To Know · Four Things A Manager Can Use

Knowing the engine explains the behavior

1 · The context window

It can only attend to what's in front of it. No document in the prompt, no knowledge of your document. Give it the right context.

2 · Examples in the prompt work

Show three examples and it follows the pattern, with no retraining. That's in-context learning, and it's exactly TabICL's trick.

3 · Length costs money

Everything looks at everything. Twice the text is about four times the comparisons. That's why long prompts are slower and cost more.

4 · Likely is not the same as true

It was trained to produce what's likely to come next. Usually that's right. Sometimes it's a confident mistake. Check what matters.

These four follow directly from how the machine works. Ask the class which one they have already run into with ChatGPT.
Part 2 · TabICL · The Problem

A spreadsheet is not a sentence

Difference 1
🔀 No natural order

Shuffle the rows or the columns and it's the same table. Shuffle a sentence and it's nonsense.

Difference 2
📏 Every column speaks its own language

"70" is a lot for support calls and normal for a monthly bill. A number means nothing without its column.

The goal
💬 The table is the prompt

Hand over rows with answers and rows without. Get predictions back in one pass. No training, no tuning.

TabICL = Tabular In-Context Learning. Built by researchers at Inria in France, open source.

Text transformers get order for free and treat every word the same way. Tables break both assumptions. TabICL's design is an answer to these two differences.
How TabICL Is Built · Three Transformers In A Row · Press The Buttons

Three stages: columns, rows, examples

Press 1, 2, 3 in order. Stage 1: attention runs down each column, so every number is read relative to its own column. Stage 2: attention runs across each row, and each customer is boiled down to one fingerprint (512 numbers in the original paper). Stage 3: the answers are attached to the labeled fingerprints, and each new row attends to the labeled rows to form its prediction. Three transformers, each using the attention idea from Part 1.

🔴 left · 🟢 stayed · ⭐ the new customer. Thicker line = more attention. The prediction is the attention-weighted vote of the labeled customers.

A simplified picture. In the real model, "similar" is judged on the learned fingerprints, across many layers, so it can weigh which columns matter and how they combine.

Drag the new customer around: its prediction follows its neighbors. Slide "focus" up and it listens only to the closest few; slide it down and it averages everyone. The real TabICL learned its own notion of "similar" during pretraining, which is what makes it far better than a plain nearest-neighbor vote.
How It Got Smart · Pretraining · Click To Reveal

It practiced on millions of made-up tables

1
Invent a world

A random cause-and-effect recipe: "age affects income, income affects churn…"

2
Generate a table

Run the recipe to make fake rows. The true answers are known.

3
Hide some answers

The model predicts them from the rows that still have answers.

4
Nudge and repeat

Small tables first (about 1,000 rows), then up to 60,000.

It never saw your data. It learned how to learn from a table.

Like a doctor who trained on thousands of textbook cases before meeting you. The original model took about 20 days on three GPUs to pretrain. You pay none of that: you just call it.

The fake tables come from random "structural causal models" plus tree-based generators. Because each fake table's true answers are known, the model can be graded and nudged, exactly like the Studio loop. Curriculum: easy (small tables) first, then larger ones.
What Happens When You Call It

Three lines of code, one pass

from tabicl import TabICLClassifier

model = TabICLClassifier()
model.fit(X_train, y_train)     # just stores your rows
model.predict_proba(X_new)      # all the thinking happens here
Inside predict

1. Your labeled rows and new rows go in together.

2. Columns → rows → examples, in one pass.

3. It repeats this on a few shuffled copies of the columns (8 by default) and averages, because a table has no natural column order.

4. Out come the chances. No knobs were trained.

That's why fit took almost no time in the Great Tabular Race: there is nothing to fit.

This is the code from the bonus Colab. The shuffled-columns averaging is a small ensemble; it restores the "column order doesn't matter" property that tables have.
Why It's Useful, And Where To Be Careful

A superb first try, with honest limits

Why you'd reach for it
  • No training, no tuning. An answer in seconds.
  • Strong on small and medium tables, where neural networks used to lose. Its makers report beating heavily tuned XGBoost-style models on about 80% of the TabArena benchmark datasets.
  • Open source, runs on your machine. Your data doesn't have to leave the building.
  • The current version handles yes/no, categories and numbers (classification and regression).
Where to be careful
  • Size: benchmarked from about 300 to 100,000 rows. Rows cost grows fast: twice the rows, about four times the work.
  • Wide tables: accuracy can slip beyond roughly 100 columns.
  • Text columns are treated as categories. It doesn't read their meaning.
  • Explaining it is harder than explaining a checklist.
  • Benchmarks come from its makers. Test it on your own data against XGBoost.
Balanced view. For a class project with a few thousand rows it is an excellent first model. For millions of rows, heavy text, or a regulator who wants every decision explained, start elsewhere and compare.
