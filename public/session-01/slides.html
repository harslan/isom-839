The Grammar You'll Use Every Week

Every model has three ingredients

1 · Decision Variables
What can we control?

How many to make, where to ship, whom to schedule, what to charge.

2 · Objective
What does "best" mean?

Maximize profit. Minimize cost. Minimize seconds on a velodrome.

3 · Constraints
What are the rules?

Capacity, budget, demand, physics, policy. Reality, written as inequalities.

From a lemonade stand to Microsoft's cloud — same grammar, bigger sentences.

Live · Let's Build One Together

Sawyer Furniture Co.

  • Tables: $120 profit · 4 hrs carpentry · 2 hrs finishing
  • Chairs: $80 profit · 3 hrs carpentry · 1 hr finishing
  • This week: 240 carpentry hours, 100 finishing hours
  • Write down your production plan and its profit. Don't overthink — 60 seconds.
The Same Problem, In Model Grammar

The formulation

# Decision variables
T = number of tables,  C = number of chairs

# Objective
maximize  120·T + 80·C

# Constraints
4·T + 3·C  ≤  240    (carpentry hours)
2·T + 1·C  ≤  100    (finishing hours)
T, C  ≥  0

Three ingredients. That's a complete linear program.

And In Python · gurobipy

Eleven lines to optimal

import gurobipy as gp
from gurobipy import GRB

m = gp.Model('sawyer_furniture')
T = m.addVar(name='tables')
C = m.addVar(name='chairs')
m.setObjective(120*T + 80*C, GRB.MAXIMIZE)
m.addConstr(4*T + 3*C <= 240)
m.addConstr(2*T + 1*C <= 100)
m.optimize()
print(T.X, C.X, m.ObjVal)
What You Just Bought

A certificate of optimality

A spreadsheet gives you an answer. A solver gives you the provably best answer — and can tell you exactly what one more hour of carpentry is worth.
