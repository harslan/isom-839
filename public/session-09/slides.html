7:45 – 7:55 · Opening Story

Hands up: who has cancelled a subscription… and come back?

What you did

Cancelled Netflix when your show ended. Came back for the next season. Maybe twice.

What the company sees

Not "lost," but a state: paused. Winning back a lapsed customer usually costs a fraction of finding a new one.

Tonight's question

If I know which state each customer is in today, where will they be in a year, and what is that worth?

Why This Question Matters

Your biggest asset isn't on the balance sheet. And it leaks every month.

1 · Diagnose
Where is each customer today?

Two companies, same revenue. One is mostly Active, the other mostly At-Risk. Only the second is in trouble, and revenue can't see it yet.

2 · Predict
Where will they be in a year?

Monthly losses compound, and people don't feel compounding. "80% stay" sounds healthy. The math tells a different story.

3 · Price
What is that worth?

Once a customer has a dollar value, you can make decisions about her instead of guessing.

Revenue tells you what happened. States tell you what's about to happen.

What A Dollar Value Unlocks

Price a customer, and four decisions open up

Acquisition
A spending ceiling

If a customer is worth $193, a $250 campaign to win one loses money, however good it looks.

Retention
A target

Which customers to save, and when. Tonight's twist: spend where they wobble.

Valuation
What the company is worth

Investors judge subscription businesses largely on how their customers stay, not on this quarter's sales.

Planning
Real numbers

Revenue forecasts, server capacity and hiring all start from "how many customers next year?"

Same question, everywhere: banks (credit ratings: BBB → default) · hospitals (patients) · factories (machines) · HR (employees) · Google (web pages)

You can't choose the best action until you can price where it leads. Tonight we price it. On Nov 18 we choose.

7:55 – 8:05 · Round 1 · The Markov Property

The goldfish rule: only where you are now matters

A board game

In Chutes & Ladders, your next square depends on your square and the spin. Not on how you got there.

A customer

Next month depends on this month's state. Not on her whole history.

"Too simple!"

Then put more into the state: "Active, year 1" vs "Active, year 3". Choosing good states is the art.

The math name: the Markov property. A bold shortcut, and often good enough to make money.

States Come From Behavior Data

Four states, one monthly shuffle

Active: used it this month · At-Risk: using it half as much · Lapsed: paused, pays $0 · Churned: cancelled for good

Each arrow is a chance: ".30" = 30 of every 100 At-Risk customers bounce back to Active next month.

The Transition Matrix · P

One table holds the whole story

Read a row out loud: "Of every 100 active customers, 80 stay, 15 wobble, 3 go quiet, 2 leave."

Every row must add up to 1, because everyone has to go somewhere. Check that first. It is the most common Markov bug in real work.

8:20 – 8:35 · Round 2 · Steady State · Click A Starting Point

Leave it running: the chain forgets where it started

A made-up Boston coffee market

Each month, 90% of Dunkin' regulars stay and 10% switch. Starbucks regulars: 80% stay, 20% switch.

Pick a start. Then pick another.

Every start lands on ⅔ Dunkin'. Why? It settles when the flows balance: Starbucks loses fans twice as fast, so Dunkin' ends up with twice as many.

That resting point is the steady state. Long-run market share comes from switching rates, not today's share.

Round 2 · Absorbing States

Everyone churns eventually

Churned is a one-way door (math name: an absorbing state). Leave the chain running and everyone ends up there.

Like a leaky bucket: every bucket empties. That is not news. What matters is how much water comes out first.

For customers: how many months do they pay before they leave?

Round 3 · The Shortcut

The average, without rolling a single die

Warm-up: one state

Stay 80% each month, nowhere else to go?

1 ÷ (1 − 0.8) = 5 months

Many states: same idea, matrix-sized

N = (I − Q)−1

Q = the table without Churned. I = the matrix version of the number 1.

In plain words: N counts the months a customer spends in each state, on average.
